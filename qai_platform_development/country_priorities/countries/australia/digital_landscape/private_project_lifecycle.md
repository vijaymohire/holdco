# Australian Private-Sector Project Lifecycle

**Status:** Designed / Documented — project-specific applicability and implementation to be verified
**Owner:** Bhadale IT — QAI / FAEP
**Scope:** Australian private-sector projects, including enterprise technology, industrial systems, research collaborations, product development, and commercialization
**Last reviewed:** 3 October 2026

## 1. Purpose

Define a reference lifecycle for identifying commercial opportunities, validating customer needs, developing business cases, designing and delivering solutions, managing commercial and technical risks, and establishing sustainable operations.

The lifecycle supports Bhadale IT's assessment of private-sector opportunities for QAI / FAEP products, services, research, modernization, and industry pilots.

It is intended to support startups, enterprise customers, technology partners, research collaborators, and industry-specific initiatives such as the Digital Farm CPS / Digital Twin pilot.

**Important:** This is a proposed project-management reference model, not a mandatory Australian industry standard. Actual obligations depend on the business, sector, jurisdiction, contracts, funding arrangements, and intended use of the solution.

## 2. Objectives

The lifecycle aims to:

* Establish customer problems and commercial opportunities before committing substantial resources.
* Validate demand, feasibility, affordability, and differentiation.
* Connect customer requirements to architecture, product scope, delivery plans, and acceptance criteria.
* Protect background intellectual property and clarify ownership and licensing of project outputs.
* Support staged investment, experimentation, and controlled product development.
* Manage supplier, partner, security, privacy, safety, and operational risks.
* Establish measurable customer value, revenue assumptions, and whole-of-life costs.
* Provide evidence for decisions to continue, modify, commercialize, license, partner, or stop.

## 3. Reference Lifecycle

The following stages provide a common structure. Small projects may combine stages; complex projects may require additional reviews, specialist assurance, or repeated discovery and validation.

| Stage | Name                                          | Principal output                                               |
| ----- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| P0    | Opportunity identification                    | Opportunity statement and initial hypothesis                   |
| P1    | Customer and market discovery                 | Validated problem evidence and stakeholder needs               |
| P2    | Commercial feasibility                        | Options, market assessment, costs, risks, and value hypothesis |
| P3    | Business case and investment decision         | Approved scope, funding assumptions, and success measures      |
| P4    | Contracting, IP, and partnership arrangements | Agreed commercial and legal boundaries                         |
| P5    | Architecture and solution design              | Baseline design and implementation plan                        |
| P6    | Prototype or minimum viable solution          | Demonstrable solution within a defined scope                   |
| P7    | Verification and customer validation          | Test evidence and customer acceptance findings                 |
| P8    | Deployment and operational transition         | Operational solution and support arrangements                  |
| P9    | Commercial launch and service delivery        | Defined offer, delivery model, and customer support            |
| P10   | Value measurement and optimization            | Measured results and improvement backlog                       |
| P11   | Scale, license, partner, pivot, or exit       | Approved next-stage commercial decision                        |

These identifiers are internal reference labels, not prescribed industry gates.

## 4. Stage Definitions

### P0. Opportunity Identification

**Purpose:** Identify a problem, unmet need, or potential commercial opportunity.

Activities:

* Identify a target customer, industry, or user group.
* Describe the problem and its operational or financial consequences.
* Identify existing alternatives and relevant market developments.
* Connect the opportunity to relevant QAI / FAEP capabilities.
* Record assumptions, dependencies, and initial risks.
* Establish an initial hypothesis about customer value.

Outputs:

* Opportunity statement.
* Target customer profile.
* Initial problem and value hypothesis.
* Preliminary capability mapping.

**Decision:** Is the opportunity sufficiently defined to justify discovery?

### P1. Customer and Market Discovery

**Purpose:** Determine whether the problem is real, important, and supported by credible customer evidence.

Activities:

* Conduct customer and stakeholder interviews where access is available.
* Examine industry information, competing products, substitutes, and procurement patterns.
* Document current workflows and operational constraints.
* Identify the intended user, buyer, budget owner, and relevant decision-makers.
* Establish baseline measures where data is available.
* Record willingness-to-test, willingness-to-pay, and purchasing assumptions separately from confirmed demand.

Outputs:

* Customer discovery record.
* Market and competitor assessment.
* Current-state workflow.
* Initial requirements catalogue.
* Demand assumptions and supporting evidence.

**Decision:** Is there enough evidence to justify feasibility work?

An expression of interest, positive discussion, survey response, or policy alignment is not by itself evidence of a signed contract or established market demand.

### P2. Commercial Feasibility

**Purpose:** Assess whether a viable solution and delivery model may be achievable.

Activities:

* Compare build, buy, reuse, license, and partnership options.
* Assess technical feasibility and dependencies.
* Estimate development, infrastructure, deployment, support, and maintenance costs.
* Examine pricing models and potential revenue sources.
* Assess legal, contractual, IP, security, privacy, safety, and sector-specific constraints.
* Identify delivery capabilities, suppliers, and potential partners.
* Establish a classical or existing-solution baseline where relevant.
* Document uncertainty and conditions that would change the assessment.

Outputs:

* Feasibility assessment.
* Options comparison.
* Initial cost and revenue model.
* Risk and dependency register.
* Proposed validation approach.

**Decision:** Is there a credible path to testing the commercial and technical assumptions?

### P3. Business Case and Investment Decision

**Purpose:** Establish the scope, resources, economics, and conditions for proceeding.

Activities:

* Define target outcomes, scope, exclusions, and milestones.
* Estimate capital expenditure, operating expenditure, and whole-of-life costs.
* Document expected revenue, customer value, and benefits assumptions.
* Assess cash flow, funding needs, delivery risk, and resource constraints.
* Define measurable success criteria and decision thresholds.
* Identify the consequences of delaying, changing, or stopping the project.
* Approve an initial budget or staged funding limit.

Outputs:

* Business case or project proposal.
* Delivery and investment plan.
* Cost and value assumptions.
* Milestone and acceptance criteria.
* Investment decision record.

**Decision:** Is the proposed investment authorised, affordable, and supported by sufficient evidence for the next stage?

Forecasts must be identified as forecasts, not presented as achieved revenue, savings, valuation, or customer benefits.

### P4. Contracting, IP, and Partnership Arrangements

**Purpose:** Establish the rights, obligations, and boundaries needed before material collaboration or delivery begins.

Activities:

* Identify background IP contributed by each party.
* Identify third-party IP, open-source software, licences, and usage restrictions.
* Define ownership and permitted use of newly created project IP.
* Agree confidentiality, publication, disclosure, and data-handling arrangements.
* Define deliverables, acceptance, payment, liability, warranties, support, and termination terms.
* Establish licensing scope, field of use, territory, exclusivity, sublicensing, and milestone provisions where relevant.
* Confirm who is authorised to approve commitments and disclosures.
* Obtain legal or specialist advice where required.

Outputs:

* IP and asset register.
* Approved confidentiality arrangements where appropriate.
* Signed contract, licence, or collaboration agreement.
* Responsibility and dependency matrix.
* Defined commercial and disclosure boundaries.

**Decision:** Are the relevant parties authorised and contractually ready to proceed?

Do not assume that paying for a project automatically transfers all IP rights. Ownership and usage rights depend on the applicable law and contractual arrangements.

### P5. Architecture and Solution Design

**Purpose:** Convert agreed customer requirements into a design that can be implemented and tested.

Activities:

* Define requirements, interfaces, system boundaries, and architecture decisions.
* Identify reusable components and existing systems.
* Define data flows, access controls, and integration dependencies.
* Select appropriate deployment environments.
* Address performance, reliability, security, privacy, safety, accessibility, and operational needs.
* Define testing methods, acceptance criteria, and fallback behaviour.
* Establish configuration and change control.

Outputs:

* Solution architecture.
* Requirements and interface specifications.
* Implementation plan.
* Risk and assurance plan.
* Verification strategy.

### P6. Prototype or Minimum Viable Solution

**Purpose:** Test defined assumptions with the smallest appropriate implementation.

Activities:

* Build or configure the agreed prototype or minimum viable solution.
* Use synthetic, sample, or authorised operational data as appropriate.
* Record the implementation environment and execution mode.
* Implement the minimum necessary integrations.
* Track defects, limitations, dependencies, and deviations.
* Avoid unnecessary exposure of confidential or proprietary information.

Outputs:

* Prototype or minimum viable solution.
* Demonstration record.
* Implementation and configuration evidence.
* Known-limitations register.
* Initial test results.

**Decision:** Does the solution justify further validation or development?

A prototype demonstrates only the functions and conditions actually tested. It does not automatically establish production readiness or commercial viability.

### P7. Verification and Customer Validation

**Purpose:** Evaluate the solution against defined requirements and customer outcomes.

Activities:

* Verify functional and non-functional requirements.
* Test integrations, reliability, security, and performance as appropriate.
* Compare results with the agreed baseline.
* Obtain structured feedback from intended users.
* Record defects, deviations, risks, and outstanding acceptance conditions.
* Evaluate the costs and benefits demonstrated so far.
* Assess whether advanced AI or quantum workloads provide measurable value over appropriate alternatives.

Outputs:

* Verification and validation report.
* Baseline comparison.
* Customer feedback and acceptance findings.
* Updated risk and defect register.
* Recommendation to continue, revise, or stop.

**Decision:** Have the agreed validation criteria been met, and is further investment justified?

Do not claim quantum advantage without suitable comparative evidence. Do not claim independent validation unless an independent assessment has actually occurred.

### P8. Deployment and Operational Transition

**Purpose:** Prepare the accepted solution for its intended operating environment.

Activities:

* Confirm deployment scope and authorised environment.
* Configure access, monitoring, backup, recovery, and support.
* Complete required security, privacy, safety, and operational checks.
* Prepare documentation and training.
* Establish incident, change, release, and escalation procedures.
* Confirm data handling, service responsibilities, and licence conditions.
* Obtain the necessary customer approvals.

Outputs:

* Deployment record.
* Operational handover.
* Support and escalation arrangements.
* Service-level measures where agreed.
* Outstanding-action register.

### P9. Commercial Launch and Service Delivery

**Purpose:** Establish a repeatable way to sell, deliver, support, and maintain the product or service.

Activities:

* Define the target market, product scope, pricing, and sales model.
* Establish delivery and support responsibilities.
* Document customer onboarding, configuration, and acceptance procedures.
* Manage licences, subscriptions, renewals, and supplier dependencies.
* Maintain product documentation, release records, and support channels.
* Track sales, customer adoption, service quality, and delivery costs.

Outputs:

* Defined commercial offer.
* Sales and delivery documentation.
* Customer onboarding and support model.
* Product or service performance metrics.
* Commercial and operational records.

A product being available for demonstration is not equivalent to a commercially launched or supported product.

### P10. Value Measurement and Optimization

**Purpose:** Determine whether the solution delivers the expected customer and business value.

Activities:

* Compare actual performance with the agreed baseline.
* Measure adoption, customer outcomes, revenue, costs, and service quality.
* Review incidents, defects, feedback, and operational constraints.
* Identify improvement opportunities and technical debt.
* Reassess supplier and partner performance.
* Update the business case and forecast using observed evidence.

Outputs:

* Value and performance report.
* Updated cost and revenue assumptions.
* Improvement backlog.
* Updated risk register.
* Evidence-based continuation recommendation.

### P11. Scale, License, Partner, Pivot, or Exit

**Purpose:** Decide how to proceed after evaluating commercial and operational evidence.

Potential pathways include:

* Scale direct product or service delivery.
* License IP to an established industry participant.
* Enter a joint development or commercialization partnership.
* Establish a dedicated operating or commercialization entity.
* Expand into a new industry or jurisdiction.
* Modify the product, target customer, or business model.
* Pause or stop the project.
* Transfer or exit an asset under approved contractual and legal arrangements.

Activities:

* Review customer demand, unit economics, delivery capacity, and risk.
* Assess the funding and working capital required for growth.
* Review IP ownership, licensing scope, and partner obligations.
* Establish the governance and operating model for the next stage.
* Define measurable milestones and conditions for further investment.

Outputs:

* Scale-up or commercialization proposal.
* Updated business case.
* Approved partnership, licensing, or operating model.
* Investment and milestone decision.
* Closure or transition records where applicable.

**Decision:** Is the next stage supported by sufficient evidence, resources, contractual rights, and risk controls?

## 5. Commercial Decision Gates

Use these decision gates to control expenditure and avoid advancing solely because work has already started.

| Gate                 | Question                                             | Typical evidence                                                       |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| D0 — Opportunity     | Is the problem sufficiently defined?                 | Opportunity statement and initial assumptions                          |
| D1 — Demand          | Is there credible evidence of customer need?         | Interviews, operational data, market research, or customer commitments |
| D2 — Feasibility     | Is a viable solution and delivery model plausible?   | Options, technical assessment, cost assumptions, and risk register     |
| D3 — Investment      | Is the next stage authorised and affordable?         | Business case, budget, milestones, and decision record                 |
| D4 — Contract and IP | Are rights and obligations clear?                    | Executed agreement or other approved arrangements                      |
| D5 — Prototype       | Does the defined solution work in the tested scope?  | Demonstration record and test evidence                                 |
| D6 — Validation      | Does it meet agreed acceptance criteria?             | Comparative test results and customer findings                         |
| D7 — Launch          | Can it be operated and supported as agreed?          | Deployment evidence and operational procedures                         |
| D8 — Scale           | Does evidence justify further commercial investment? | Measured outcomes, cost model, demand evidence, and approved plan      |

These are proposed internal controls. They do not replace contractual approvals or statutory requirements.

## 7. Intellectual Property and Confidentiality Controls

Apply IP controls throughout the lifecycle rather than only at launch.

Maintain separate records for:

* **Background IP:** Existing IP brought into the project by each party.
* **Project IP:** IP created during the project.
* **Third-party IP:** IP owned by external parties and used under applicable terms.
* **Confidential information:** Information subject to contractual or other restrictions.
* **Open-source components:** Components subject to their respective licence conditions.

Before sharing sensitive technical details, confirm the purpose, recipient, permitted use, confidentiality arrangements, and disclosure authority.

Before starting collaborative development, define who may use, modify, commercialize, license, publish, or sublicense relevant IP. Keep any transfer or licensing rights consistent with the signed agreement.

Reference: [IP Australia — IP management for collaborations](https://www.ipaustralia.gov.au/manage-my-ip/how-to-commercialise-my-ip/commercialisation-and-collaboration/ip-management-for-collaborations).

## 8. Initial Application: Digital Farm CPS / Digital Twin

Use the Digital Farm pilot as a bounded example of the lifecycle.

1. Identify a specific farm operation or measurable agricultural problem.
2. Document stakeholders, existing assets, workflows, and available data.
3. Establish the classical baseline and current operating costs where data permits.
4. Define the smallest useful pilot and its acceptance criteria.
5. Assess relevant privacy, security, safety, data, and IP requirements.
6. Choose simulation, emulation, or physical execution based on the experiment.
7. Build and demonstrate the defined scope.
8. Compare results with the baseline and record limitations.
9. Assess customer value, implementation cost, and operational needs.
10. Decide whether to revise, extend, commercialize, license, partner, or stop.

Do not infer customer demand from the existence of the architecture or pilot design. Record actual stakeholder feedback, agreements, and test results separately.

## 9. Project Evidence Register

For each material stage or decision, record:

* Project ID and stage.
* Customer or stakeholder requirement.
* Requirement and decision IDs.
* Project owner and decision authority.
* Scope and acceptance criteria.
* Applicable legal, contractual, and technical obligations.
* Budget, cost assumptions, and funding approval.
* IP and confidentiality boundaries.
* Evidence references and evidence dates.
* Current status, limitations, and unresolved risks.
* Next action, responsible owner, and due date.
* Decision outcome and review trigger.

Use evidence appropriate to the claim: market research for market assumptions, signed agreements for contractual commitments, test reports for technical performance, and financial records for realised revenue and costs.
