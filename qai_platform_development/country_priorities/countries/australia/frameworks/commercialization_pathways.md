# Commercialization Pathways

**Status:** Designed / Documented — pathway selection and commercial readiness to be verified
**Scope:** Australian and international commercialization of QAI / FAEP products, services, research outputs, and industry solutions.
**Last reviewed:** 3 October 2026

## 1. Purpose

Define the pathways through which QAI / FAEP capabilities can progress from research, intellectual property, and documented designs into validated demonstrations, pilots, partnerships, products, services, and sustainable commercial operations.

This framework supports decisions about:

* Which capabilities are sufficiently evidenced for external engagement.
* Whether to develop internally, collaborate, license, partner, or establish a dedicated commercial entity.
* How to validate customer needs and willingness to adopt.
* How to fund development, demonstrations, production, and market entry.
* How to preserve intellectual property ownership and define permitted use.
* How to measure technical progress, commercial viability, and customer value.
* How to expand from an initial demonstrator into repeatable products and services.

This document defines a decision framework. It does not establish that a particular product is market-ready, that a funding program is available, or that a proposed venture is eligible for Australian support.

## 2. Commercialization Principles

1. **Evidence before scale:** Validate a defined problem and demonstrate relevant capabilities before committing to large-scale production or investment.
2. **Customer need before product expansion:** Identify the buyer, user, problem, existing alternatives, budget context, and measurable outcome.
3. **Reuse before rebuilding:** Reuse established QAI / FAEP components where their suitability and readiness have been verified.
4. **Stage-gated investment:** Release resources against agreed technical, customer, commercial, and risk milestones.
5. **Portfolio-level IP protection:** Define which rights are made available for a specific product, pilot, or venture without assuming transfer of the wider portfolio.
6. **Separate evidence from ambition:** Distinguish planned functionality, implemented capability, demonstration results, and independently validated outcomes.
7. **Commercial viability:** Evaluate implementation costs, support obligations, sales cycles, working capital, and recurring operating expenses alongside potential revenue.
8. **Responsible deployment:** Address security, privacy, safety, data governance, applicable standards, and human oversight.
9. **Jurisdiction-aware execution:** Verify the legal, tax, business-registration, funding, and procurement requirements applicable to the selected operating model.
10. **Learn and adapt:** Use customer feedback, pilot results, and commercial evidence to determine whether to continue, revise, partner, or stop.

## 3. Commercialization Lifecycle

Use the following stages as a common pathway. A project may repeat or combine stages where justified, but stage transitions should have recorded evidence and approval.

| Stage                      | Objective                                                           | Required evidence or decision                                                                               |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Research and discovery  | Identify a problem, opportunity, or technical concept               | Problem statement, initial research, assumptions, and IP inventory                                          |
| 2. Capability definition   | Define the intended product or service                              | Scope, architecture, users, requirements, dependencies, and limitations                                     |
| 3. Feasibility             | Determine whether the proposed solution can reasonably be developed | Technical feasibility, risk assessment, baseline, resource estimate, and alternatives                       |
| 4. Prototype or simulation | Test the concept in a controlled environment                        | Prototype, simulation, test cases, recorded results, and known limitations                                  |
| 5. Proof of concept        | Test whether the core approach works for a defined scenario         | Agreed success criteria, reproducible evidence, and comparison with a baseline                              |
| 6. Pilot                   | Test the solution in a bounded customer or operational context      | Pilot scope, permissions, operational controls, acceptance criteria, and outcome report                     |
| 7. Commercial validation   | Determine whether customers will adopt and pay for the offering     | Customer feedback, buyer requirements, pricing assumptions, delivery model, and cost model                  |
| 8. Productization          | Make the offering repeatable and supportable                        | Product specification, release process, documentation, security review, support model, and quality controls |
| 9. Market entry            | Begin controlled commercial delivery                                | Sales channel, contractual terms, onboarding, pricing, support, and delivery capacity                       |
| 10. Scale and improvement  | Expand validated delivery and improve economics                     | Repeatable delivery, customer outcomes, unit economics, reliability, and investment plan                    |

**Stage-gate rule:** Progression requires evidence appropriate to the stage. A successful simulation does not by itself demonstrate production readiness, customer acceptance, or quantum advantage.

## 4. Commercialization Pathway Catalogue

### 4.1 Internal development and direct commercialization

Develop, own, market, and deliver a product or service directly.

**Typical requirements:**

* Sufficient engineering, product management, and delivery capacity.
* Funding for development, testing, operations, support, and market entry.
* Clear ownership or usage rights for the necessary IP.
* A defined customer segment and distribution or sales approach.
* A plan for security, maintenance, updates, and product lifecycle management.

This pathway provides direct control over product development and customer relationships while placing more delivery and market risk on the operating business.

### 4.2 Licensing intellectual property

Permit a third party to use specified IP under a written licence while retaining ownership of the licensed IP, subject to the actual agreement.

Potential terms include:

* Defined products, technical components, fields of use, and territories.
* Exclusive, sole, or non-exclusive rights.
* Upfront fees, milestone payments, royalties, or minimum payments.
* Development and commercialization milestones.
* Quality requirements, reporting, audit rights, and sublicensing conditions.
* Improvements, derivative works, background IP, and foreground IP.
* Term, termination, post-termination obligations, and rights reversion where agreed.

Licensing can support commercialization when a partner has manufacturing, distribution, capital, or market access that the IP owner does not currently have.

### 4.3 Strategic partnership or joint development

Work with an established business, research organisation, university, integrator, or industry participant.

Before work begins, document:

* Each party's responsibilities, funding, personnel, and deliverables.
* Access to data, facilities, hardware, and software.
* Background IP contributed by each party.
* Ownership and licensing of new results and improvements.
* Publication, confidentiality, security, and data-sharing rules.
* Acceptance criteria, dispute resolution, and exit arrangements.

A partnership does not automatically grant either party ownership of the other's pre-existing IP.

### 4.4 Dedicated product company or spin-off

Establish or use a separate operating entity to develop, market, and deliver a defined product or business line.

The arrangement may involve a licence from the IP owner, an assignment of specifically identified assets, investment in the operating entity, or another documented structure.

Define:

* The entity's permitted product and market scope.
* IP ownership and licensing arrangements.
* Capital contributions, investor rights, governance, and reporting.
* Founder responsibilities, management roles, and decision rights.
* Product development and commercialization milestones.
* Revenue allocation, royalties, dividends, or other agreed returns.
* Exit, acquisition, change-of-control, and licence-continuity provisions.

A new entity should not be assumed to own the whole QAI / FAEP portfolio merely because it commercializes one component.

### 4.5 Services-led commercialization

Generate revenue through architecture, research, assessment, integration, modernization, implementation, training, or managed services where the relevant service capability is available.

Services may help establish customer relationships and reveal repeatable requirements that can later inform product development.

Separate reusable background IP and general methods from customer-specific deliverables in proposals and contracts.

### 4.6 Pilot-led commercialization

Use a bounded pilot to establish technical suitability, operational feasibility, customer value, and implementation requirements.

A pilot should define:

* A specific customer problem and agreed scope.
* Baseline performance or the existing process.
* Available data, access permissions, and test conditions.
* Success and acceptance criteria.
* Cost, schedule, responsibilities, and operational constraints.
* Evidence to be produced and the method for evaluating results.
* Exit, continuation, and production-transition conditions.

A pilot is a validation activity, not an automatic commitment to production procurement or further funding.

### 4.7 Research collaboration and technology transfer

Collaborate with research institutions, universities, industry laboratories, or specialist partners to develop and validate technology.

Clarify research objectives, publication rights, IP ownership, background IP access, foreground IP arrangements, confidentiality, funding conditions, and commercial licensing before substantive work begins.

### 4.8 Acquisition, merger, or strategic investment

Consider acquisition, merger, or investment when there is a defined commercial rationale, a suitable counterparty, and sufficient evidence to support valuation and due diligence.

Review IP title, ownership records, licences, contracts, technical evidence, liabilities, customer commitments, and the consequences of any proposed change of control.

An acquisition or investment in an operating entity should not be treated as an automatic transfer of assets that the entity does not own.

## 6. Initial Demonstrator: Digital Farm CPS / Digital Twin

Use the Digital Farm demonstrator as a candidate initial commercialization pathway, subject to confirming the current implementation and evidence.

### Initial scope

* Farm, field, greenhouse, and related asset representations.
* Domain services such as crop, water, asset, inventory, workforce, and economic functions.
* Interfaces to relevant sensors, data sources, enterprise systems, and external services.
* Sense → Process → Decide → Act → Learn workflows.
* Simulation, emulation, virtualization, and physical execution where each is available and verified.
* Resource, safety, security, governance, and value-management controls.

### Proposed validation sequence

1. Define one specific agricultural problem and participating stakeholder.
2. Document the existing process and classical baseline.
3. Inventory the assets, data, interfaces, and operating constraints.
4. Demonstrate the smallest useful workflow using available functionality.
5. Record test results and compare outcomes with the baseline.
6. Validate findings with the stakeholder.
7. Identify essential gaps, delivery costs, and operational requirements.
8. Decide whether to improve the demonstrator, conduct a bounded pilot, partner with an established supplier, or stop.

### Potential outcome measures

Select measures appropriate to the actual use case, such as:

* Time required to complete a defined workflow.
* Data completeness and freshness.
* Forecast or decision accuracy against a defined reference.
* Water, energy, labour, or resource use where measurable.
* Error rates, service availability, and recovery time.
* Usability, adoption, and stakeholder acceptance.
* Total cost of delivery and estimated ongoing operating cost.

Do not claim improvements until measurements support them. Do not claim quantum advantage unless appropriate comparative testing establishes the relevant advantage for the specific workload.

## 7. Commercial and Financial Model

Assess each offering using a documented financial model appropriate to its stage.

### Cost categories

* Research and engineering effort.
* Hardware, cloud, hosting, software, and data services.
* Testing, validation, security, assurance, and compliance.
* IP protection, legal advice, contracts, and licensing administration.
* Sales, marketing, customer acquisition, and partner costs.
* Implementation, training, maintenance, and customer support.
* Insurance, accounting, administration, and applicable taxes.
* Working capital, contingency, and ongoing product investment.

### Revenue models to evaluate

* Fixed-price projects and implementation fees.
* Time-based professional services.
* Subscription or usage-based software fees.
* Hosting and managed-service fees.
* Hardware or infrastructure sales where applicable.
* Licensing fees, milestone payments, and royalties.
* Research contracts and funded collaborations where eligible.
* Joint ventures, investment, or other agreed commercial arrangements.

For each proposed model, record the customer, price basis, delivery cost, payment timing, recurring obligations, gross-margin assumptions, and sensitivity to changes in demand or cost.

Do not treat projected revenue, portfolio estimates, prospective grants, or indicative valuations as realized income or independently verified value.

## 8. Australian Market Entry and Local-Value Evidence

Build Australian market readiness progressively.

Potential workstreams include:

* Identifying Australian customers, industry partners, research organisations, and integrators.
* Verifying the appropriate legal and business structure for the proposed activity.
* Confirming applicable registration, tax, insurance, employment, and contracting obligations.
* Building evidence of actual Australian research, engineering, employment, manufacturing, services, and supply-chain participation.
* Assessing Commonwealth, NSW, ACT, and other relevant procurement or partnership pathways.
* Understanding data sovereignty, privacy, cybersecurity, and sector-specific requirements.
* Documenting support arrangements, delivery capacity, and customer references as they become available.

Do not represent a foreign-developed product as Australian-made, or claim Australian ownership, local content, SME status, funding eligibility, or supplier qualification without evidence satisfying the applicable definition.

## 9. Funding and Commercialization Support

Assess funding and support opportunities only after defining the project, intended outcome, applicant entity, location, funding requirement, and delivery plan.

Potential categories include:

* Government business advisory services.
* Research and industry collaboration programs.
* Competitive grants and challenge-based programs.
* Private investment, strategic partnerships, and venture funding.
* Customer-funded pilots and commercial contracts.
* Industry or regional development partnerships.

For each program, verify its current status, applicant eligibility, eligible costs, co-funding requirements, IP conditions, deadlines, and assessment criteria through the responsible authority.

A historical program description does not establish that applications are currently open. For example, the official Industry Growth Program page currently states that the program is paused to new applications. Check the latest program status before planning around it.

## 10. Intellectual Property and Portfolio Protection

Before a commercialization discussion, maintain an IP inventory that distinguishes:

* Existing background IP and its owner.
* Publicly disclosed material.
* Confidential know-how, source code, designs, models, and research records.
* Registered IP rights and their jurisdictional status.
* Unregistered rights and relevant supporting records.
* Third-party and open-source dependencies.
* New work proposed for a pilot, partnership, or commercial entity.

For each arrangement, specify the rights required for the defined purpose, the permitted field of use and territory, sublicensing conditions, fees or royalties, improvements, confidentiality, termination, and post-termination rights.

Prefer clearly scoped licences when they meet the commercial objective and preserve the intended ownership structure. Consider assignment or broader transfer only after evaluating its consequences and obtaining appropriate legal and commercial advice.

## 11. Commercialization Decision Record

For each proposed pathway, maintain a decision record containing:

* Opportunity and capability identifiers.
* Customer problem and intended outcome.
* Current capability and evidence status.
* Proposed pathway and rationale.
* Target users, buyers, partners, and market assumptions.
* IP ownership and rights required.
* Development, delivery, and operating costs.
* Funding source and eligibility status, if relevant.
* Delivery, regulatory, security, and commercial risks.
* Milestones, success criteria, and decision dates.
* Responsible owner and approval.
* Decision: proceed, proceed conditionally, partner, defer, or stop.

The decision should reflect the evidence available at the time and identify unresolved assumptions.

## 12. Performance and Learning

Track stage-appropriate indicators rather than relying only on the number of products, documents, or potential IP items.

Examples include:

* Number of capabilities with reproducible demonstration evidence.
* Number of customer problems validated with relevant stakeholders.
* Pilot acceptance against pre-agreed criteria.
* Time and cost to deliver a defined use case.
* Conversion from validated pilot to paid engagement, where applicable.
* Delivery quality, support burden, and customer feedback.
* Revenue, cash flow, recurring costs, and customer concentration.
* IP licensing performance and contractual milestone completion.
* Gaps closed and lessons incorporated into the product roadmap.

Report actual results separately from forecasts and targets.

## 13. Governance and Review

Review this framework when:

* A new product or market is proposed.
* A pilot reaches a new stage gate.
* A commercial partner, investor, or licensee is considered.
* A material change in IP ownership or licensing is proposed.
* Funding or procurement requirements change.
* Technical evidence changes the readiness assessment.
* A delivery, security, contractual, or commercial issue reveals a process gap.

Maintain links to supporting evidence and record material decisions. Obtain qualified legal, tax, financial, and regulatory advice where required.
