# Public Needs Framework — Australia

**Status:** In Progress
**Purpose:** Establish a repeatable method for identifying, validating, prioritising, and addressing public needs through relevant QAI / FAEP capabilities.
**Last reviewed:** 3 October 2026
**Maintainer:** Bhadale IT / QAI-FAEP
**Scope:** Australian communities, public institutions, businesses interacting with government, service users, public-sector employees, and relevant industry ecosystems.

---

## 1. Objectives

This framework defines how Bhadale IT identifies public needs and determines whether QAI / FAEP capabilities can address them responsibly, practically, and measurably.

The objectives are to:

1. Identify needs experienced by individuals, communities, businesses, public institutions, and service-delivery organisations.
2. Distinguish documented needs from assumptions, policy signals, and technology-led hypotheses.
3. Understand the people affected, their circumstances, existing services, and barriers to access.
4. Connect validated needs to specific problems, desired outcomes, capabilities, and evidence.
5. Prioritise opportunities according to public value, urgency, feasibility, risk, and measurable benefit.
6. Identify where existing services and systems should be reused, connected, or improved before introducing new technology.
7. Establish appropriate stakeholder participation, accessibility, privacy, security, safety, and governance controls.
8. Support bounded pilots with measurable acceptance criteria and clear continuation or stopping decisions.
9. Feed validated findings into Australian national priorities, jurisdictional alignment, sector priorities, product development, and procurement planning.

**Core principle:** Start with the need experienced by people—not with a product, technology, or predetermined solution.

The Australian Government's Digital Service Standard places people and businesses at the centre of digital service design and delivery. Its criteria include understanding users, inclusion, connecting services, building trust, avoiding unnecessary reinvention, monitoring services, and continuous improvement. Applicability should be checked against the relevant policy and service.
Official source: https://www.digital.gov.au/policy/digital-experience/digital-service-standard

## 2. What counts as a public need?

A public need is a documented or reasonably evidenced condition affecting people's ability to access services, participate in economic or community life, remain safe, obtain reliable information, or achieve an important outcome.

A need may be experienced directly by the public or indirectly by the organisations and workers who provide public services.

| Need category                | Examples to investigate                                                                   | Potential evidence                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Access and inclusion         | Difficulty accessing services, digital exclusion, language or accessibility barriers      | User research, accessibility reviews, service feedback              |
| Service quality              | Delays, repeated submissions, confusing processes, inconsistent information               | Service metrics, complaints, journey mapping                        |
| Community resilience         | Disruption to essential services, local infrastructure vulnerabilities                    | Incident records, risk assessments, community consultation          |
| Agriculture and food systems | Water constraints, crop monitoring needs, workforce coordination, supply-chain visibility | Farm interviews, operational records, pilot measurements            |
| Energy and utilities         | Asset visibility, demand management, maintenance coordination                             | Asset data, outage records, operator interviews                     |
| Infrastructure and transport | Maintenance backlogs, fragmented asset information, planning coordination                 | Asset registers, service data, engineering assessments              |
| Environment and water        | Resource monitoring, environmental change, water-use visibility                           | Monitoring data, environmental assessments                          |
| Business and industry        | Administrative burden, fragmented information, technology adoption barriers               | Business consultation, workflow analysis                            |
| Public-sector workforce      | Repetitive work, disconnected tools, difficult knowledge retrieval                        | Staff research, process observations, task analysis                 |
| Research and innovation      | Difficulty validating emerging technologies or translating research into operational use  | Research consultations, test results, technology readiness evidence |
| Digital trust and safety     | Privacy concerns, cyber risks, unclear automated decisions                                | Risk assessments, user feedback, security reviews                   |
| Regional development         | Service accessibility, infrastructure coordination, local skills and capability needs     | Regional data, stakeholder consultation, local plans                |

These categories are an initial discovery structure, not a claim that every issue is present in every Australian community.

## 3. Public needs, policy priorities, and technology opportunities

These concepts must be recorded separately.

| Concept                | Meaning                                                                     | Example of appropriate evidence                                          |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Policy priority        | An outcome or area identified in an official strategy                       | Current government policy or strategy                                    |
| Public need            | A condition affecting people or organisations                               | User research, credible data, documented service barriers                |
| Problem statement      | A clearly bounded description of the condition and its consequences         | Baseline, affected users, process evidence                               |
| Solution hypothesis    | A proposed way to address the problem                                       | Rationale, assumptions, alternatives                                     |
| QAI / FAEP capability  | A specific product, service, or technical function proposed for evaluation  | Technical specification and implementation evidence                      |
| Validated outcome      | A measured change attributable to the intervention to an appropriate degree | Baseline comparison, evaluation results, stakeholder assessment          |
| Commercial opportunity | A feasible route to fund, procure, adopt, or sustain the solution           | Confirmed buyer or sponsor, delivery model, cost and contract assessment |

A policy may identify a strategic priority without identifying a specific local problem. A stakeholder may experience a problem that does not require AI or quantum technology. A technology demonstration may work technically without producing sufficient public value to justify deployment.

Each of these possibilities must remain open during assessment.

### 4.3 Evidence quality

Prioritise evidence according to its relevance, reliability, recency, and relationship to the affected population.

Potential evidence includes:

* Official statistics and published service reports.
* Legislation, policies, strategies, and formal service commitments.
* Public consultations and stakeholder submissions.
* Interviews with affected users and service-delivery staff.
* Complaints, service requests, and process-performance data.
* Field observations and operational records.
* Independent research and credible sector studies.
* Pilot measurements and controlled technical evaluations.

For each source, record the publication date, population or geography covered, method, limitations, and whether the evidence is direct or indirect.

A national statistic should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of a specific local problem. A stakeholder interview is valuable but does not necessarily establish how widespread a problem is.

## 5. Stakeholder and affected-user model

Identify who experiences the need, who provides the service, who makes decisions, and who bears the risks or costs.

| Stakeholder group                                 | Questions to investigate                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Individuals and service users                     | What are they trying to achieve? What prevents them from doing so?                                    |
| Communities and representative organisations      | Which groups are affected? Are experiences different across locations or circumstances?               |
| Public-sector frontline staff                     | Where do delays, manual work, repeated handling, or service failures occur?                           |
| Agency management and service owners              | Which outcomes, service levels, and risks must be managed?                                            |
| Businesses and industry operators                 | What operational or regulatory friction affects participation and productivity?                       |
| Technical and security teams                      | What integration, data, security, resilience, and operational constraints apply?                      |
| Researchers and domain specialists                | What is already known? Which assumptions or technical claims require validation?                      |
| Procurement, finance, and legal teams             | What delivery, funding, contracting, privacy, and liability conditions apply?                         |
| Delivery partners                                 | What existing systems, services, skills, and infrastructure could be reused?                          |
| People potentially excluded or adversely affected | Could the proposed change create accessibility, affordability, privacy, safety, or fairness concerns? |

Do not assume that the person commissioning a service, the person operating it, and the person affected by it have identical needs.

## 6. Public-needs discovery method

Apply this process to each candidate problem.

### Step 1 — Identify the need

Describe the situation in plain language. Avoid beginning with a preferred product or technology.

### Step 2 — Identify affected people

Specify the users, communities, organisations, and locations involved. Record who may be excluded from the available evidence.

### Step 3 — Understand the current experience

Map the existing service, workflow, infrastructure, or operating model. Identify workarounds, repeated effort, delays, costs, and failure points.

### Step 4 — Establish the baseline

Collect available evidence of current performance. Record what is known, what is estimated, and what remains unknown.

### Step 5 — Validate the problem

Use interviews, consultation, field observation, credible datasets, or operational records to test whether the problem exists and whether it matters to the affected stakeholders.

### Step 6 — Identify root causes and alternatives

Determine whether the problem results from process design, policy, skills, data quality, infrastructure, coordination, or technology. Consider non-digital and lower-complexity alternatives.

### Step 7 — Define the desired outcome

Specify the change that would benefit the affected users. Make it measurable where practical.

### Step 8 — Map capabilities

Assess whether existing tools, services, integrations, or QAI / FAEP capabilities could contribute to the outcome.

### Step 9 — Assess risks and feasibility

Review technical, financial, legal, privacy, cybersecurity, accessibility, safety, operational, and environmental considerations as applicable.

### Step 10 — Decide the next action

Record whether to conduct further discovery, test a solution hypothesis, prepare a pilot, refer the issue to an existing service, defer it, or close the investigation.

**Working sequence:** Identify → Understand → Validate → Define outcomes → Map capabilities → Assess → Demonstrate → Evaluate.

## 7. Problem statement and needs register

Use a consistent structure for each identified need.

**Recommended problem statement:**

> [Affected group] experiences [specific difficulty] in [context or location], resulting in [observable consequence]. Available evidence indicates [baseline or supporting evidence]. The main uncertainties are [knowledge gaps]. The desired outcome is [measurable improvement], subject to [important constraints].

Avoid claims such as “AI will solve the problem” before the underlying need, causes, and alternative approaches have been assessed.

### Recommended register fields

| Field                              | Description                                                                    |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Need ID                            | Unique internal identifier                                                     |
| Need title                         | Short, neutral description                                                     |
| Jurisdiction and location          | Geographic and administrative context                                          |
| Affected users                     | People and organisations experiencing the need                                 |
| Need category                      | Service, access, infrastructure, agriculture, environment, workforce, or other |
| Problem statement                  | Current condition and consequence                                              |
| Evidence references                | Sources, dates, data, and stakeholder records                                  |
| Baseline                           | Current measurable state, if available                                         |
| Root-cause hypotheses              | Candidate explanations requiring assessment                                    |
| Existing services and alternatives | What is already available or could be reused                                   |
| Desired outcome                    | Change sought by affected users                                                |
| Potential harms and constraints    | Privacy, safety, accessibility, cost, and other risks                          |
| Stakeholder validation             | Status and supporting record                                                   |
| Candidate capabilities             | Relevant QAI / FAEP or external capabilities                                   |
| Technical readiness                | Evidence-based capability status                                               |
| Opportunity maturity               | Current validation stage                                                       |
| Priority rationale                 | Transparent explanation of why further work is warranted                       |
| Owner and next action              | Accountability and follow-up                                                   |
| Decision and review date           | Proceed, investigate, defer, or close                                          |

Use neutral descriptions. Avoid recording unnecessary personal information or sensitive information about individuals. Apply appropriate consent, access controls, retention, and privacy requirements to research records.

## 8. Prioritisation framework

Prioritisation should help allocate limited discovery and pilot resources transparently. It must not substitute for stakeholder validation or mandatory legal and safety requirements.

Assess each candidate need against the following dimensions:

| Dimension               | Assessment question                                                                  |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Public value            | What meaningful benefit could result, and for whom?                                  |
| Evidence strength       | How reliable and directly relevant is the evidence?                                  |
| Urgency                 | What are the consequences of delay or inaction?                                      |
| Equity and inclusion    | Who benefits, who may be excluded, and who may be harmed?                            |
| Feasibility             | Can the problem be addressed within available technical and operational constraints? |
| Reuse potential         | Can existing services, systems, infrastructure, or standards be reused?              |
| Cost and sustainability | Are implementation, operation, maintenance, and transition costs understood?         |
| Risk                    | What safety, security, privacy, legal, environmental, and delivery risks exist?      |
| Measurability           | Can the outcome be evaluated against a credible baseline?                            |
| Stakeholder commitment  | Is there a relevant stakeholder willing to participate in validation or a pilot?     |

Record the evidence and rationale for each assessment. Do not create a high numerical score merely because a need aligns with an emerging technology or government strategy.

Mandatory constraints and unacceptable risks should be treated as gates, not offset by a high score in another category.

### Capability status vocabulary

Use these statuses consistently:

* **Planned** — Intended but not yet designed in sufficient detail.
* **Designed / Documented** — Architecture or requirements documented; implementation not established.
* **Partially Implemented** — Some functionality exists, with known gaps.
* **Implemented — Verification Pending** — Implementation exists, but adequate verification evidence is not yet recorded.
* **Demonstrated** — Functionality has been shown in a defined environment.
* **Validated** — Results have been assessed against predefined acceptance criteria.
* **Not Assessed** — Evidence is insufficient to determine status.
* **Not Applicable** — The capability is not relevant to the need under assessment.

Do not use “validated” as a synonym for “demonstrated,” and do not equate a successful technical test with a proven public benefit.

## 10. Initial application: Digital Farm public-needs discovery

The Digital Farm CPS / Digital Twin pilot is a starting point for applying this framework to a bounded domain. It should test a specific operational need before expanding to a wider ecosystem.

Potential discovery topics include:

* Crop and field monitoring.
* Water-use visibility and resource coordination.
* Farm asset and inventory tracking.
* Workforce and workflow coordination.
* Operational data integration.
* Cost, yield, quality, or resource-efficiency measurement where suitable data exists.
* Decision support and the safe escalation of uncertain recommendations.

These are hypotheses for discovery, not confirmed needs for a particular Australian farm or region.

### Suggested pilot sequence

1. Select one clearly bounded operational problem with a willing stakeholder.
2. Document the current workflow and available data.
3. Establish a classical baseline and measurable acceptance criteria.
4. Identify data ownership, consent, security, and operational constraints.
5. Demonstrate only the required functionality using available simulation, emulation, or real data as appropriate.
6. Compare the proposed approach with the baseline.
7. Record usability, technical performance, costs, limitations, and stakeholder feedback.
8. Decide whether to improve, extend, repeat, or stop the pilot.

Quantum or hybrid computation should be evaluated only where there is a defined technical hypothesis and a fair comparison with suitable classical methods. The use of quantum terminology alone is not evidence of advantage or public value.

## 11. Public value and outcome measurement

For each pilot, define outcomes before execution. Select measures that match the problem and the affected users.

Potential measures include:

| Outcome dimension    | Example measures                                                      |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Access               | Completion rate, successful access, accessibility barriers identified |
| Service performance  | Processing time, waiting time, rework, error rate                     |
| Reliability          | Availability, failed operations, recovery time                        |
| Workforce            | Manual effort, task completion time, training needs                   |
| Resource use         | Water, energy, materials, or compute consumption                      |
| Economic value       | Cost per completed task, operating cost, measurable benefit           |
| Quality              | Accuracy, completeness, consistency, defect rate                      |
| Inclusion            | Differences in outcomes across relevant user groups                   |
| Trust and safety     | Reported incidents, review outcomes, inappropriate automated actions  |
| Environmental impact | Relevant measured resource or environmental indicators                |
| Adoption             | User participation, task success, continued use, feedback             |

For each measure, record the definition, baseline, target, collection method, reporting period, and known limitations.

Avoid claiming causation from a simple before-and-after comparison where other factors may explain the result. Where practical, use a suitable comparison method and document the assumptions.

## 12. Governance, safeguards, and participation

Public-needs work should apply safeguards proportionate to the people, data, decisions, and operating environment involved.

### 12.1 People and inclusion

* Engage affected users early and throughout the design process.
* Consider accessibility, digital literacy, language, connectivity, and non-digital service channels.
* Provide clear information about the purpose and limits of a proposed service.
* Preserve appropriate human review and appeal or escalation pathways for consequential decisions.
* Assess whether benefits and burdens are distributed fairly.

### 12.2 Data and privacy

* Collect only information needed for a defined purpose.
* Establish lawful authority, consent where required, ownership, access, retention, and sharing arrangements.
* Assess data quality, representativeness, provenance, and potential bias.
* Protect confidential, personal, and sensitive information.
* Verify applicable privacy and data-handling requirements before collecting or transferring data.

### 12.3 AI, automation, and quantum-related work

* Define the intended use and prohibited uses.
* Identify the consequences of incorrect or unavailable outputs.
* Document model, algorithm, and system limitations.
* Provide monitoring, escalation, rollback, and incident-handling arrangements as appropriate.
* Evaluate AI or quantum-related techniques against suitable baselines.
* Avoid automated action where the risk, uncertainty, or authority to act has not been adequately assessed.

### 12.4 Transparency and accountability

* Record assumptions, decisions, and evidence.
* Make the purpose and limitations of demonstrations clear.
* Separate experimental research from operational services.
* Define responsibilities among the buyer, supplier, operator, and delivery partners.
* Ensure claims about outcomes and capability match the evidence.

## 13. Relationship to procurement and commercialization

The Public Needs Framework should inform, but not replace, procurement and commercial assessments.

| Public-needs finding                                       | Follow-up pathway                                             |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Need not yet confirmed                                     | Continue discovery or close the hypothesis                    |
| Need exists but an adequate solution is already available  | Consider reuse, integration, referral, or process improvement |
| Need confirmed but proposed solution is unsuitable         | Investigate alternatives                                      |
| Need confirmed and technical feasibility remains uncertain | Define a research or feasibility experiment                   |
| Need and feasibility are sufficiently established          | Assess a bounded pilot                                        |
| Pilot produces useful validated results                    | Assess operational readiness, cost, support, and adoption     |
| Buyer requirements and supplier eligibility are verified   | Consider the relevant procurement or commercial pathway       |
| Risks or value do not meet agreed criteria                 | Modify, defer, or stop the work                               |

Before entering a procurement, confirm the actual buyer, requirements, participation conditions, contractual terms, funding or purchasing mechanism, and IP implications. Public need does not itself create a procurement opportunity or guarantee funding.

## 14. Initial implementation plan

### Phase 1 — Establish discovery inputs

* Maintain an authoritative source register.
* Identify priority public-service and sector domains.
* Define the stakeholder interview and consultation approach.
* Establish a reusable needs register and evidence-record format.

### Phase 2 — Validate candidate needs

* Start with a small number of bounded problems.
* Identify affected users and relevant service owners.
* Collect available evidence and document its limitations.
* Validate the problem and current-state baseline.

### Phase 3 — Map and assess capabilities

* Map the validated problem to existing services and alternatives first.
* Identify relevant QAI / FAEP capabilities.
* Assess implementation status, feasibility, dependencies, and risks.
* Record gaps and required evidence.

### Phase 4 — Demonstrate and evaluate

* Select a pilot with a willing stakeholder and clearly defined scope.
* Agree on outcomes, acceptance criteria, data arrangements, and responsibilities.
* Run the demonstration or pilot.
* Record results and compare them with the baseline.

### Phase 5 — Decide and learn

* Assess public value, usability, cost, risk, and operational sustainability.
* Record whether to continue, improve, scale, or stop.
* Update the needs register, gap analysis, product alignment, and procurement planning.

## 16. Completion criteria

This framework can move from **In Progress** to an operational needs-discovery process when:

* [ ] Authoritative sources are recorded and reviewed.
* [ ] Candidate needs are distinguished from policy signals and solution hypotheses.
* [ ] Affected users and relevant stakeholders are identified.
* [ ] Evidence quality and limitations are recorded.
* [ ] Validated problem statements and baselines are available for selected needs.
* [ ] Existing services and non-technology alternatives have been considered.
* [ ] QAI / FAEP mappings include evidence-based readiness statuses.
* [ ] Public value, risks, and measurable outcomes are defined.
* [ ] Pilot decisions use explicit acceptance criteria.
* [ ] Findings are reflected in product, sector, gap, and procurement registers.

---

**Summary:** The Public Needs Framework provides a traceable route from public evidence and stakeholder experience to validated problems, measurable outcomes, suitable capabilities, and responsible pilot decisions. It keeps people and public value at the centre while ensuring that technology claims, readiness, and commercial opportunities are supported by evidence.
