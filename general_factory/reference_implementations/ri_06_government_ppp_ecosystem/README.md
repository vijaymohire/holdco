# RI-06 — Government Capability-to-Project Ecosystem

## 1. Purpose

RI-06 defines a proposed, technology-neutral reference model for connecting **government and public-sector outcomes** with programs, funding pathways, procurement mechanisms, experts, SMEs, research organisations, industry partners, intellectual property (IP), infrastructure, shared assets and project-based delivery.

The model explores how a federated ecosystem can assemble the capabilities needed for a defined outcome without assuming that every capability must be permanently owned, staffed or operated by a single organisation.

HoldCo is positioned as a potential **capability-to-outcome orchestration layer** that helps discover, assess, assemble, coordinate and reuse relevant capabilities under an appropriate governance and commercial arrangement.

The model is intended for assessment through suitable engagements, demonstrations and pilots. It does not imply government endorsement, an established government relationship, procurement eligibility, funding approval, a PPP award or validated economic benefits.

## 2. Design Principles

1. **Outcome before structure** — define the public need and intended results before selecting an organisational or delivery structure.
2. **Evidence-led capability matching** — assess capability relevance, availability, readiness, cost, constraints and supporting evidence.
3. **Appropriate procurement pathway** — determine the applicable procurement, grant, contribution, partnership or PPP mechanism rather than presuming a particular route.
4. **Federated participation** — assemble suitable public, private, academic, community and research participants where permitted and appropriate.
5. **Accountability by design** — establish ownership, decision rights, responsibilities, reporting and escalation before execution.
6. **Proportionate governance** — apply controls appropriate to the jurisdiction, funding arrangement, risk, data sensitivity and project scope.
7. **Transparent resource economics** — distinguish asset ownership, access rights, allocation authority, actual consumption and cost responsibility.
8. **Project-based mobilisation** — configure a delivery team and resource footprint around an approved project, subject to legal and operational requirements.
9. **Traceability and assurance** — preserve evidence connecting the need, requirements, decisions, expenditure, delivery and outcomes.
10. **Reuse with permission** — reuse eligible capabilities, methods, configurations and assets while respecting IP, confidentiality, licensing and contractual restrictions.
11. **Public value and value for money** — assess outcomes, costs, risks, alternatives and distribution of benefits rather than relying on unsupported benefit claims.
12. **No assumed entitlement** — ecosystem participation does not establish eligibility for funding, procurement, contracts, licences or government support.

## 3. Reference Workflow

The end-to-end workflow is:

**Public Outcome → Problem Definition → Program and Funding Assessment → Capability Discovery → Capability and Partner Matching → Procurement or PPP Pathway → Governance and Delivery Configuration → Project Authorisation → Mobilisation and Delivery → Outcomes Measurement → Reuse, Scale or Closure**

### Stage 1 — Public outcome and problem definition

- Identify the public-sector need, affected stakeholders and intended outcome.
- Establish the baseline, scope, constraints and measurable success criteria.
- Identify the accountable sponsor or authorised decision-maker.
- Separate confirmed requirements from assumptions and exploratory opportunities.

### Stage 2 — Program and funding assessment

- Identify potentially relevant programs, budget sources or funding mechanisms.
- Record the source, eligibility conditions, deadlines and decision authority where known.
- Assess whether the proposed work requires public funding, private investment, customer payment or a combination.
- Do not treat an identified funding program as an approved funding commitment.

### Stage 3 — Capability discovery

Identify potentially relevant capabilities across:

- Government departments and public institutions.
- SMEs, established businesses and specialist suppliers.
- Universities, research organisations and laboratories.
- Professional experts and delivery teams.
- IP, software, data, models and technical methods.
- Physical infrastructure, facilities, equipment and cloud resources.
- Regional organisations, communities and economic-development participants.
- Existing programs, projects and reusable assets.

A catalogue entry is a discovery lead, not proof of availability, qualification, ownership or suitability.

### Stage 4 — Capability and partner matching

Evaluate candidate capabilities against:

- Required functions and performance.
- Evidence of relevant experience and readiness.
- Availability and mobilisation time.
- Geographic, operational and technical constraints.
- Security, privacy, safety and data-handling requirements.
- IP ownership, licensing and usage rights.
- Cost, funding restrictions and lifecycle obligations.
- Conflicts of interest and applicable eligibility conditions.
- Dependencies, integration effort and delivery risk.

Document the rationale for selection, rejection or further assessment.

### Stage 5 — Procurement or PPP pathway

Determine the lawful and appropriate engagement mechanism for the jurisdiction and opportunity. Potential mechanisms may include:

- Open or competitive procurement.
- Approved supplier or framework arrangements.
- Research or innovation collaborations.
- Grants or contribution agreements.
- Consortia or joint delivery arrangements.
- Public-private partnership (PPP) structures.
- Other legally authorised contracting or partnership mechanisms.

These are possible pathway categories, not a statement that every pathway is available for every opportunity.

The relevant authority must determine the applicable route, eligibility, competition requirements, approvals, contracting terms and evaluation process. Capability matching must not be used to circumvent competition, supplier qualification, conflict-of-interest controls or public accountability.

### Stage 6 — Governance and delivery configuration

Define the proposed delivery ecosystem:

- Accountable public-sector sponsor or contracting authority.
- Program or portfolio governance.
- Project owner and delivery lead.
- Participating organisations and named responsibilities.
- Specialist tracks, working groups and technical contributors.
- Shared resources and asset allocation arrangements.
- Commercial, contractual and IP relationships.
- Assurance, reporting, risk and escalation processes.
- Deliverables, milestones, acceptance criteria and exit conditions.

The configuration must reflect the actual approved agreement and decision rights. A conceptual HoldCo role does not confer authority to commit public funds or bind participating organisations.

### Stage 7 — Project authorisation and mobilisation

Before delivery begins:

- Confirm the applicable approval and contract or funding instrument.
- Baseline the scope, schedule, budget and acceptance criteria.
- Verify access rights, resource availability and required permissions.
- Assign accountable owners and delivery responsibilities.
- Establish data, security, privacy, safety and records-management controls.
- Record dependencies, risks, assumptions and unresolved decisions.

Mobilisation must not be represented as authorised merely because a project configuration or proposal has been prepared.

### Stage 8 — Delivery and assurance

- Execute the agreed work packages.
- Track milestones, expenditure, resources, changes and risks.
- Maintain traceability from requirements to deliverables and acceptance evidence.
- Manage supplier, partner and stakeholder dependencies.
- Escalate material deviations through the agreed governance structure.
- Preserve records required by the contract, funding instrument and applicable law.

### Stage 9 — Outcomes measurement and disposition

Assess delivery against the approved baseline and intended public outcomes.

Possible measures include:

- Delivery against agreed scope and milestones.
- Actual cost compared with the approved budget.
- Service performance and quality.
- Time required to mobilise capabilities.
- Resource utilisation and reuse.
- Adoption, accessibility and stakeholder outcomes.
- Risk and assurance performance.
- Demonstrated economic or public value, where measurable.

Distinguish outputs delivered from outcomes observed. Where attribution is uncertain, report the limitations. Decide whether to improve, repeat, scale, transfer, maintain or close the initiative.

- Requirements and acceptance criteria.
- Capability and partner assessment.
- Funding and procurement pathway assessment.
- Proposed ecosystem and responsibility model.

## 6. Logical Interface Contracts

The following interfaces describe the information exchanged between the logical stages. They are technology-neutral contracts, not claims of implemented APIs.

| Interface | Required information | Expected result |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome intake | Need, sponsor, scope, constraints and desired outcomes | Recorded and classified opportunity |
| Program and funding assessment | Jurisdiction, potential mechanism, eligibility criteria and evidence | Assessment with conditions and gaps |
| Capability discovery | Required functions, capability catalogue and search constraints | Candidate capability set |
| Capability assessment | Requirements, evidence, readiness, availability and risk | Documented suitability assessment |
| Pathway assessment | Opportunity details, applicable rules and authority requirements | Proposed lawful pathway and approval dependencies |
| Ecosystem configuration | Roles, responsibilities, resources, dependencies and governance | Proposed delivery configuration |
| Project authorisation | Approved scope, budget, agreement, decision rights and conditions | Authorised or rejected mobilisation decision |
| Resource allocation | Approved demand, access rights, capacity, cost and constraints | Allocation decision and resource record |
| Delivery reporting | Milestones, expenditure, risks, deliverables and evidence | Status and exception records |
| Outcome assessment | Baseline, targets, actual results and attribution limits | Evidence-based outcome assessment |
| Reuse or closure | Acceptance, residual obligations, permissions and lessons | Reuse, transfer, scale or closure record |

Each interface should define its data owner, validation rules, permissions, status transitions, error conditions and audit requirements before implementation.

## 7. Logical Roles and Accountability

The model distinguishes coordination from legal authority.

- **Public-sector sponsor or authorised authority:** defines the need, exercises the decision rights assigned to it and approves commitments within its authority.
- **Program or portfolio governance:** aligns initiatives with strategic priorities, manages dependencies and oversees performance.
- **HoldCo orchestration function:** may coordinate capability discovery, ecosystem configuration, shared-resource planning and project-level integration where contracted or otherwise authorised.
- **Project owner:** remains accountable for the responsibilities assigned under the governing arrangement.
- **Delivery organisations:** provide agreed services, deliverables, resources and evidence.
- **Specialist experts and working groups:** provide defined technical, professional, research or assurance contributions.
- **Funding or investment participants:** participate under documented funding, investment or contribution terms.
- **Procurement, legal, finance and assurance functions:** perform the controls and approvals required by the applicable arrangements.
- **Independent evaluators or assurance providers:** assess evidence where required and appropriately independent.

For every project, identify who recommends, approves, executes, verifies and accepts each material decision. These roles may not be combined where separation or independence is required.

## 8. Governance, Procurement and Public-Sector Controls

A real implementation must assess the requirements applicable to its jurisdiction, funding instrument, participants and scope.

Relevant control areas may include:

- Procurement rules, competition and supplier eligibility.
- Funding conditions, allowable expenditure and reporting.
- Conflicts of interest, ethics and anti-corruption controls.
- Contract management and delegated authority.
- Privacy, personal information and data sovereignty.
- Cybersecurity, information classification and access management.
- Safety, environmental and sector-specific obligations.
- Accessibility, inclusion and stakeholder consultation.
- IP ownership, background IP, project IP, licensing and publication rights.
- Records retention, auditability and disclosure obligations.
- Tax, employment, insurance, professional registration and licensing.
- Subcontracting, consortium and partner accountability.
- Performance management, dispute resolution and termination.
- Exit, transition, continuity and asset disposition.

This reference is not legal advice or a compliance certification. The applicable authority and qualified advisers must confirm requirements before a live engagement proceeds.

## 9. Resource and Economic Model

RI-06 connects to RI-05 — Dynamic Asset Allocation — when projects require shared or scarce resources.

The model separates:

1. **Ownership:** who owns or controls an asset or capability.
2. **Access rights:** who is permitted to use it and under what conditions.
3. **Allocation authority:** who may reserve or assign it.
4. **Consumption:** what was actually used and measured.
5. **Cost responsibility:** which party bears each agreed cost.
6. **Outcome accountability:** who is responsible for the promised deliverable or result.

Potential economic categories include discovery and assessment, engineering, integration, licensing, infrastructure, operations, support, assurance and lifecycle costs. Actual charging, cost recovery, revenue sharing and payment milestones must be defined in the applicable agreement.

The model may be used to test whether capability reuse, shared resources or project-based mobilisation reduce duplication or improve utilisation. Such benefits remain hypotheses until measured against an appropriate baseline, with assumptions and attribution limitations recorded.

## 10. Risk and Dependency Management

Each proposed engagement should record, at minimum:

- Unvalidated need or unclear outcome.
- Ineligible or unavailable funding.
- Incorrect or unapproved procurement pathway.
- Incomplete capability evidence.
- Supplier or partner failure.
- Unclear authority or accountability.
- Resource unavailability or integration failure.
- Cost escalation or funding restrictions.
- IP, privacy, security or data-rights issues.
- Schedule and interdependency risks.
- Conflicts of interest or governance weaknesses.
- Inadequate outcome measurement.
- Failure to meet acceptance criteria.
- Unclear transition, continuity or closure arrangements.

Each material risk should have an owner, likelihood and impact assessment where appropriate, mitigation, review date and escalation path.

## 11. Validation and Acceptance Criteria

A reference implementation should be assessed against observable evidence.

### Minimum validation criteria

- A defined need and intended outcome can be recorded.
- Requirements and constraints can be traced to candidate capabilities.
- Candidate selection includes documented evidence and suitability criteria.
- Funding and procurement assumptions are separated from confirmed decisions.
- Roles, responsibilities and decision rights are explicit.
- Resource ownership, access, allocation and cost responsibility are distinguishable.
- Project authorisation is a separate, verifiable decision.
- Deliverables can be traced to requirements and acceptance evidence.
- Risks, dependencies and material changes are recorded.
- Outcomes can be compared with an agreed baseline where data permits.
- Closure or reuse respects contractual, legal, IP and data restrictions.

### Evidence expectations

Evidence may include approved records, decision logs, requirements matrices, capability assessments, signed agreements where applicable, test results, acceptance records, expenditure reports and outcome evaluations.

A completed template or successful demonstration of a workflow is not, by itself, proof of procurement compliance, operational effectiveness, economic benefit or government adoption.

## 13. Minimal Technology-Neutral Example

**Scenario:** A public authority identifies a need to improve access to a regional service. The required capabilities may include service design, software development, local delivery partners, data analysis and hosting infrastructure.

1. Record the problem, target population, baseline and desired outcome.
2. Confirm the responsible authority and applicable funding and procurement constraints.
3. Discover candidate capabilities and assess evidence, availability, cost and risk.
4. Evaluate the permissible procurement or partnership pathways.
5. Configure the proposed project, governance, roles, resources and acceptance criteria.
6. Obtain the required approvals and contractual authorisation.
7. Mobilise the approved resources and execute the agreed work.
8. Measure delivery and service outcomes against the baseline.
9. Document lessons and determine whether any assets or methods can lawfully be reused.

This is an illustrative workflow, not an actual government engagement, funding commitment, procurement result or claim of measured benefit.

## 14. Relationship to HoldCo Strategy

RI-06 supports the proposed HoldCo operating principle:

**National or Market Need → Opportunity → Capability Discovery → Ecosystem Assembly → Project → Resource Allocation → Outcome → Reuse or Scale**

The model explores how existing people, IP, technology, infrastructure, organisations, capital and projects may be connected around validated outcomes. HoldCo's potential role is coordination and integration within the authority granted by each engagement, not automatic ownership of all participating capabilities or control over public-sector decisions.

The long-term hypothesis is that improved discovery, coordination, resource utilisation and reuse may help reduce avoidable duplication and enable more effective delivery. The hypothesis requires validation through measurable pilots and comparisons against appropriate baselines.
